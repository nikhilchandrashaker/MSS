01 — The premise

Not "what's the highest stat" — "where would they actually play"

Every Mario Super Sluggers character has four ratings on a 1–10 scale: Pitch, Bat, Field, and Run. The obvious way to guess a position is to look at whichever number is biggest. This project deliberately doesn't do that.

Instead, each character gets a weighted fit-score for all nine real defensive positions, using weights built around the actual defensive spectrum scouts think in — catcher and shortstop are glove-first jobs where a light bat is tolerated; first base and left field are bat-first jobs where a poor glove is tolerated. The position with the highest eligible score (see the throwing-arm rule below) becomes that character's real-life projected position.

The point of the project is the disagreements. Mario's raw stats are highest in Bat and Run — the naive read says "bat-first corner guy." The weighted model instead lands him in Left Field, because his profile balances just well enough across all three offense-relevant stats that no single corner-power slot fits him as cleanly. That gap between "obvious stat" and "the model's projection" is the whole reason this dataset exists.

03 — Derived indices

Beyond the raw four

Three extra numbers sit alongside the raw ratings, all built from the idea that real offensive and defensive value isn't a single stat in isolation:

  • Offensive Index — 0.75 × Bat + 0.25 × Run. Speed turns into extra bases and stolen bases, so it counts toward offense, just less than the bat itself.
  • Defensive Index — 0.70 × Field + 0.30 × Run. Range in the field depends on instincts and hands as much as raw speed.
  • Athletic Index — the average of Pitch, Field, and Run. A rough "tool grade" that deliberately excludes hitting, the way scouts separate the hit tool from everything else.

A caveat worth flagging up front: MSS's Bat rating is a single combined offensive number — power and contact folded together — unlike real scouting, which grades Hit and Power separately. Two characters with an identical Bat score could be a slap hitter or a three-outcome slugger in real life, and this dataset has no way to tell them apart. That's a real limitation of the translation, not something the model below tries to solve.

04 — The position-fit model

Nine formulas, one per position

Pitcher and Catcher are the "battery" — the only two positions where the Pitch stat matters:

P — Pitcher 0.85 × Pitch + 0.15 × Field
C — Catcher 0.60 × Field + 0.30 × Bat + 0.10 × Pitch

The other seven positions are built entirely from Bat, Field, and Run, weighted to mirror where each sits on the real defensive spectrum — from bat-first (First Base) to pure-defense-and-speed (Center Field, Shortstop):

Why these exact numbers

Each position's weight vector was chosen so that it's mathematically possible for some stat profile to make it the single best fit. An early draft had Second Base's weights sitting exactly on the midpoint between First Base and Shortstop's — which meant 2B could never win, for any character, no matter how the raw stats came in. RF leans on Field as an arm-strength stand-in almost as much as Bat, matching how real right fielders are prized for their throwing arm. 2B leans on Run more than a straight "between 3B and SS" blend would suggest, reflecting the classic quick, contact-and-speed middle infielder.

05 — The throwing-arm rule

Why this isn't just "highest score wins"

In real baseball, left-handed throwers essentially never play Catcher, Second Base, Third Base, or Shortstop — those positions are built around a right-handed release for the throws across the diamond. The model enforces this directly: a character whose Throwing Arm is Left has their C / 2B / 3B / SS scores set to ineligible when picking an Optimal Position, even if the raw formula score there is high.

P
1B / LF / CF / RF
C · 2B · 3B · SS
Open to any throwing arm Right-handed throwers only

This is why left-handed throwers like Waluigi, Bowser Jr., Boo, Funky Kong, King K. Rool, and the whole Dry Bones family get pushed toward Pitcher, First Base, or an outfield corner in the model — even when their raw Field rating alone looks like a middle infielder's.

That's not a contradiction; it's two different questions (best fit vs. closest overall profile) answered two different ways.

07 — A finding worth flagging

Nobody on this roster is a real third baseman

Even with every position mathematically reachable, only 8 of the 9 positions actually get claimed as an Optimal Position across all 72 characters — no one projects as a true Third Baseman, and Right Field is thin too. That's a real result of the roster's stat distribution, not a modeling bug: MSS's cast skews toward pure sluggers (high Bat, low everything else → 1B), pure burners (high Run → CF), and pure defenders or pitchers, with very few characters combining strong Bat and strong Field without a big Run number — exactly the profile a hot-corner third baseman needs.

08 — Limitations

What this model can't tell you

  • Four game stats can't fully capture real baseball tools — no separate arm strength rating, no Hit/Power split, no pitch type or velocity, no plate discipline.
  • Position weights are this project's own heuristic, built to mirror the real defensive spectrum — a reasonable model, not a validated scouting formula.
  • "Field" stands in for both glove and arm strength. A character rated a poor overall fielder could still plausibly have played an arm-driven position like Right Field in reality.
  • The Mii's Throwing/Batting Arm varies by chosen gender in-game; this dataset defaults to the right-handed (male) animation for modeling purposes.
  • "Character Family" and "Color Variant" groupings are judgment calls — for example, the weapon-variant Bros (Hammer, Fire, Boomerang) are grouped together like palette-swapped color variants, even though their difference is a weapon, not just a recolor.
  • The MLB comps are illustrative pop-culture pairings on a hand-estimated 1–10 scale, not a rigorous sabermetric match to any real player's actual career stats.
